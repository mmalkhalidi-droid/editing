LESSON 3

Names and Formulas for Ionic Compounds

FOCUS QUESTION
What are the names and formulas of ionic compounds?

Formulas for Ionic Compounds

Chemists around the world need to communicate clearly with one another, so they use one standard set of rules for naming compounds. Once you know these rules, you can write a chemical formula from a compound's name, and name a compound from its formula.

An ionic compound is not made of separate molecules. It is a huge crystal of positive and negative ions arranged in a repeating pattern. Because there is no single molecule to describe, chemists use the formula unit: the chemical formula for an ionic compound, which represents the simplest ratio of the ions involved.

Example: magnesium chloride, MgCl2
The formula unit is MgCl2 because magnesium ions (Mg2+) and chloride ions (Cl−) exist in a 1 : 2 ratio. One Mg2+ (+2) is balanced by two Cl− (−1 each), so the total charge is zero.
Key idea
The total charge of a formula unit is always zero. The positive charge of the cations must exactly cancel the negative charge of the anions.

Monatomic Ions

A monatomic ion is an ion made from a single atom, such as Na+, Ca2+ or O2−. For main-group elements, the charge can be predicted from the element's group in the periodic table, because atoms gain or lose electrons to reach a stable octet (noble-gas configuration).

Transition metals (groups 3–12) often form more than one ion. Iron, for example, forms Fe2+ and Fe3+; copper forms Cu+ and Cu2+. Some, like silver (Ag+) and zinc (Zn2+), form only one common ion.

Oxidation number

The charge of a monatomic ion is called its oxidation number (or oxidation state). The oxidation number of Na+ is +1, of O2− is −2, and of Fe3+ is +3. In an ionic formula, the sum of all oxidation numbers equals zero.

Writing Formulas for Binary Ionic Compounds

A binary ionic compound contains only two elements: a metal cation and a nonmetal anion. Follow these steps:

  1. Write the symbol of the cation first, then the anion.
  2. Write each ion's charge (oxidation number).
  3. Add subscripts so that the total charge is zero. A quick method is the crisscross method: the number of each charge becomes the subscript of the other ion.
  4. Reduce the subscripts to the simplest whole-number ratio. Never write a subscript of 1.
Figure 1. The crisscross method for aluminum oxide.

Polyatomic Ions

A polyatomic ion is a group of covalently bonded atoms that carries an overall charge and behaves as a single unit, for example the sulfate ion, SO42−. The charge belongs to the whole group, not to any one atom.

Formulas with polyatomic ions

Use exactly the same charge-balancing rule. If you need more than one polyatomic ion, put it in parentheses and write the subscript outside. Never change the subscripts inside a polyatomic ion.

Calcium hydroxide
Ca2+ + 2 OH− → Ca(OH)2
Ammonium phosphate
3 NH4+ + PO43− → (NH4)3PO4
Common mistake
Writing CaOH2 instead of Ca(OH)2. Without parentheses, the 2 applies only to H, which is a different (and wrong) formula. When only one polyatomic ion is needed, as in NaNO3, no parentheses are used.

Oxyanions

An oxyanion is a polyatomic ion made of an element (usually a nonmetal) bonded to one or more oxygen atoms. Many elements form a family of oxyanions. Their names follow a pattern:

  • The ion with more oxygen atoms ends in -ate; the ion with fewer ends in -ite (nitrate NO3−, nitrite NO2−).
  • For halogens with four oxyanions, add the prefix per- (one more O than -ate) and hypo- (one fewer O than -ite).

Naming Ionic Compounds

To name an ionic compound from its formula, apply these rules in order:

  1. Name the cation first, then the anion. NaCl is sodium chloride, not chloride sodium.
  2. A monatomic cation takes the name of the element. Ca2+ is the calcium ion.
  3. A monatomic anion takes the root of the element name plus -ide. Br− is bromide; N3− is nitride.
  4. If the metal can form more than one ion, show its charge with a Roman numeral in parentheses (the Stock system). Fe2+ = iron(II); Fe3+ = iron(III).
  5. If the compound contains a polyatomic ion, simply use the polyatomic ion's name. NaOH is sodium hydroxide.
Worked example 5: name CuO
Copper can be Cu+ or Cu2+, so find its charge. Oxide is O2−, and there is one O for one Cu, so copper must be +2.
Name: copper(II) oxide
Worked example 6: name Fe2(SO4)3
Three sulfates give 3 × (−2) = −6. Two Fe ions must give +6, so each Fe is +3.
Name: iron(III) sulfate
Worked example 7: name K2CrO4 and NH4Cl
Potassium has only one ion (K+), so no Roman numeral: potassium chromate.
NH4+ is ammonium: ammonium chloride.
Summary flowchart
Is the cation a metal with more than one possible charge (most transition metals, also Sn and Pb)? → Yes: add a Roman numeral. No (groups 1, 2, Al, Zn, Ag): no Roman numeral.
Is the anion a single element? → Yes: use -ide. No: use the polyatomic ion's name.

LESSON 4

Metallic Bonds and the Properties of Metals

FOCUS QUESTION
How do metals and ionic compounds compare and contrast?

Metallic Bonds

Although metals are not ionic, they share several properties with ionic compounds. In both cases, bonding is based on the attraction of particles with unlike charges. In the solid state, metals often form lattices similar to ionic crystal lattices. In such a lattice, each metal atom is surrounded by 8 to 12 other metal atoms.

The electron sea model

Metal atoms have few valence electrons, and they hold them loosely. In a metal lattice, the outer energy levels of neighbouring atoms overlap. The valence electrons are therefore not tied to any one atom; they move freely through the whole solid. The electron sea model describes a metal as a lattice of positive metal ions (cations) immersed in a "sea" of moving valence electrons.

Electrons that are free to move and are not held by any specific atom are called delocalized electrons. A metallic bond is the attraction of a metallic cation for delocalized electrons.

Properties of Metals

The physical properties of metals can be explained by metallic bonding. These properties also give evidence about the strength of metallic bonds.

Melting and boiling points

The melting points of metals vary greatly. Mercury is a liquid at room temperature (melting point −39 °C), which makes it useful in scientific instruments such as older thermometers and barometers. Gallium melts at about 30 °C, so it can melt in your hand. Tungsten has the highest melting point of any metal, 3422 °C, so it is used in light-bulb filaments.

Boiling points of metals tend to be extremely high. Melting does not require much energy to move cations and electrons past each other; the metal stays bonded as a liquid. But boiling requires each atom to be separated completely from the electron sea and the other cations, which needs a great deal of energy. For example, copper melts at 1085 °C but boils at 2562 °C.

Malleability, ductility and durability

Metals are malleable (can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (can be drawn into wires). When a force is applied, the cations slide past each other, but the electron sea moves with them and keeps them bonded. The metal changes shape instead of breaking. In an ionic crystal, sliding brings like charges next to each other; they repel, and the crystal shatters.

Figure 3. After a layer is shifted, a metal keeps its bonding, but an ionic crystal lines up like charges.

Metals are generally durable. Their cations are strongly attracted to the delocalized electrons and are not easily removed from the metal.

Thermal and electrical conductivity

Delocalized electrons move easily through the metal. They carry electric current when a voltage is applied, and they carry heat quickly from one place to another. This is why copper is used in electrical wiring and why metal pans heat up quickly.

Luster

Delocalized electrons interact with light: they absorb and release photons. This gives metals their shiny appearance, called luster.

Hardness and strength

The more delocalized electrons there are, the stronger the metallic bonds. Alkali metals (group 1) have only one valence electron per atom, so they are soft enough to cut with a knife. Transition metals such as chromium, iron and nickel can delocalize both their outer s electrons and some inner d electrons. This produces a denser electron sea, so these metals are hard and strong. Chromium is one of the hardest elements.

Remember
Almost every property of metals (conductivity, malleability, ductility, luster, high boiling point) comes from one idea: mobile, delocalized electrons holding a lattice of cations together.

Metal Alloys

Because of the nature of metallic bonding, it is easy to add other elements to a metal. An alloy is a mixture of elements that has metallic properties. Alloys are made by melting a metal and mixing in the other elements, then letting the mixture cool. Alloys often have properties that are better than those of the pure metal; for example, they can be harder, stronger or more resistant to corrosion.

Substitutional alloy
Some atoms of the original metal are replaced by other atoms of similar size.
Examples: brass (copper + zinc), sterling silver (silver + copper), 14-karat gold.
Interstitial alloy
Small atoms fit into the holes (interstices) between the larger metal atoms.
Example: carbon steel, where small carbon atoms sit between iron atoms, making it harder and stronger than pure iron.
